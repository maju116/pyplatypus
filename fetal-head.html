A fetal head circumference: the measurement a Dice cannot see

Ultrasound, an ellipse fitted to a predicted mask, and the one page in this documentation whose answer is a length rather than a score.

HC18 is 999 two-dimensional ultrasound images of the fetal skull, each with an ellipse drawn round it by a sonographer, and a CSV giving every image’s pixel size in millimetres and the head circumference that was recorded. It is CC BY 4.0.

Every other article here ends on an overlap score. This one ends on a length in millimetres, because that is what a clinician writes down and charts against gestational age. Nobody charts a Dice.

The run below is examples/measure_fetal_head_circumference.py, and the weights it produced are published as hc18-unet. Where a number belongs to those published weights rather than to this run, it says so - the two are different draws of one recipe, and how different is itself one of the findings.

Why a circumference is not a score

A Dice asks how many pixels two masks share. A circumference asks how long the boundary of one of them is. The two fail in different directions, and the directions are the wrong way round for anybody measuring a length.

A ragged boundary barely moves an integral over a region. Add and remove pixels along an edge in roughly equal measure and the area, and the ellipse fitted to it, hardly move - while the Dice falls with every one of them.

A mask that is systematically fat or thin moves it linearly. Grow the whole outline by a pixel and the circumference grows with it, every time, in one direction - while the Dice barely notices, because a one-pixel rim is a small share of a large blob.

So the error a Dice sees best is the one that does not matter here, and the error it sees worst is the one that does. That is an argument until it is measured, and the last three sections measure it.

The data

The archive unpacks to training_set/ with an image and an _Annotation.png beside it, and training_set_pixel_size_and_HC.csv giving the pixel size and the recorded circumference.

The annotation is a drawn curve rather than a filled region - an ellipse two or three pixels wide with nothing inside it - so the region has to be filled before anything can be trained. The example does that with a hole fill before it builds the configuration, and the criterion for a usable case falls out of the fill rather than being chosen: a curve that runs off the edge of the frame encloses nothing, so the fill returns it unchanged.

186_HC, 346_HC, 628_2HC, 787_HC

Four of the 999, left out. The sonographer’s ellipse continues outside the picture, so the recorded millimetres describe a shape the file does not contain - a limitation of this instrument rather than a defect in the data, and the reason fitting a conic to the visible arc is how the literature handles it.

Splitting by examination, not by image

HC18 names its files 000_HC, 628_2HC: the leading number is the examination, and several images can come from one. Dividing at random by file puts an image of an examination in training and another image of the same examination in the test set, and the score becomes partly a memory test.

split.group_by is where that belongs. It is a regular expression over the sample key, and the key here is the file name without its extension:

split:
  fractions: [0.6, 0.2, 0.2]
  group_by: '^(\d+)_'
  seed: 1

995 usable images from 803 examinations. Three fractions rather than two, because the millimetres are reported on the third: no number on this page comes off the data early_stopping watched.

The specification

task: semantic_segmentation
data:
  train_path: measurements/hc18/cases.csv
  mode: config_file
  colormap: [[0, 0, 0], [255, 255, 255]]
  split:
    fractions: [0.6, 0.2, 0.2]
    group_by: '^(\d+)_'
    seed: 1
models:
  - name: skull
    architecture: u_net
    # 800 x 540 arrives as 800 x 544, which divides by 2^4 and resamples the data almost
    # not at all. A measurement that ends in millimetres should argue with the ellipse,
    # not with the interpolation.
    input_shape: [544, 800]
    blocks: 4
    filters: 16
    batch_size: 4
    epochs: 40
    loss:
      name: cce_dice
    metrics:
      - name: dice
        include_background: false
      - name: iou
        include_background: false
    optimizer:
      name: adam
      learning_rate: 0.001
    callbacks:
      - name: early_stopping
        monitor: val_dice
        patience: 8
        restore_best: true
    augmentation:
      # No vertical flip: an ultrasound fan has a top and a bottom, and a mirrored one is a
      # picture of nothing. The ellipse is symmetric under the horizontal one.
      - name: HorizontalFlip
        params:
          p: 0.5
      - name: RandomBrightnessContrast
        params:
          p: 0.3

Training

from pyplatypus import Engine, from_yaml

engine = Engine(from_yaml("examples/hc18.yaml"))
engine.fit()

Forty epochs at 544 x 800, about half an hour on a GTX 1070. The run this page describes reached a mean Dice of 0.9347 on its held-out third; the published hc18-unet weights are a different draw of the same recipe and reached 0.9494.

Neither number is the point of the page, which is the next three sections.

From a mask to a millimetre

A prediction is a mask on a grid. A circumference is a length in the world, and what connects them is the pixel size, which is a property of the file rather than of the model.

from pyplatypus import ellipse_circumference

for case, prediction in engine.predict_stream("skull", split="test", space="source"):
    mask = prediction.argmax(axis=-1) == 1
    mm = ellipse_circumference(mask, spacing=(pixel_mm, pixel_mm))

Two things in those four lines decide whether the number means anything.

space="source", not the model’s grid. The pixel size in the CSV describes the image as it is on disk. A prediction resized to the model’s input shape has pixels of a size nobody recorded, and multiplying by the file’s number would be wrong by the ratio of the two shapes.

spacing scales the coordinates, not the answer. For an area the two are the same thing, since a count scales by the product of the sides either way. For a length they are not: a perimeter is not separable in its axes, so there is no number to multiply a pixel-space circumference by unless the pixels happen to be square. Measured on a circle of radius 100 px at spacing=(2, 1), which is an ellipse of semi-axes 200 and 100: scaling the coordinates gives 968.9 against 968.8 integrated, where the two scalings somebody reaches for answer 628.3 and 1256.7. HC18’s pixels are square, so this example would have been right either way and would have stopped being right on the first anisotropic dataset.

ellipse_axes() returns the two axes if the shape rather than the perimeter is what is wanted, and mask_volume() and instance_sizes() are the same idea for areas and volumes.

What the annotation costs before the model does

Before blaming a model for a millimetre, measure what the pipeline costs with no model at all: put the truth masks through the same ellipse fit and compare with the sonographer’s figures.

truth masks against the chart, 200 cases      +0.692 mm
the annotation's drawn line                    2.523 px
pi * w * pixel predicts                       +0.685 mm
correlation of prediction with error            0.9994
left unexplained                               0.008 mm

The floor is biased rather than noisy - every case too large by about the same amount - and the cause is the annotation’s own line. The curve a sonographer drew is two and a half pixels wide, not one; filling it puts the region’s boundary half a width outside the curve, and a radius larger by w/2 makes a circumference larger by pi * w.

That is the difference between an explanation and a guess, and it is why the prediction is reported beside the bias rather than subtracted from it: a correction folded into the measurement would hide the one number here that is about the dataset rather than about us.

A model reproducing the truth masks perfectly would still read +0.69 mm high on this split, and that figure is not a constant of the dataset: it is pi * w converted to millimetres, so it scales with the pixel size, which HC18 records per image. The R vignette measures the same thing on its own split and gets a larger number for that reason, not a different answer. So the model’s own contribution is the comparison against the truth masks rather than against the chart, and both belong on a page that reports millimetres - the first blames the model for a drawing convention, the second hides the convention from whoever reads the number.

The Dice cannot see this error

mean Dice                                       0.9347
median absolute error                           2.00 mm
mean absolute error                             5.66 mm
75th and 90th percentile                  4.43, 12.36 mm
worst case                                     96.35 mm
Spearman between the two orderings               0.620

Of the 136 test images scoring 0.95 or better - a figure anybody would publish - 49 are still wrong by more than 2 mm, which is 36% of them. On the published weights the same count is 85 of 144, or 59%.

Read the vertical spread of the right-hand panel at any fixed Dice. A column of points two orders of magnitude tall is the finding: the score does not tell you what the measurement will be.

The median and the mean are both here because they disagree. The error distribution has a long tail - half the cases inside 2.00 mm and a tenth outside 12.36 - so the mean describes the tail rather than a typical case, and the median alone would hide that some images are out by the width of a head.

What this does not settle

One run. The same recipe trained again gives a different correlation. Four draws of it have been measured:

the run on this page                             0.620
a second                                         0.435
the published hc18-unet weights                  0.153
the R vignette's own                             0.458

All four are far from a shared ordering and they span a factor of four. The direction is the finding; the magnitude is one draw, and a single number quoted from this page would overstate the case at one end.

from pyplatypus import available_weights

available_weights()["hc18-unet"]

A measurement is not a measuring instrument. The worst case here is out by 96 mm. These weights are not a biometric tool with a person looking at them, let alone without.

The ellipse is the shape assumed. ellipse_circumference() fits one, which is right for a fetal skull and wrong for anything that is not an ellipse - and it measures one object per image, which is HC18’s shape rather than the general one. Every object in an image, measured separately, is #272 and is not built.

Notes on the machine

A GTX 1070, which wants the CUDA 12 line of PyTorch; device_report() says what the card can do and pick_device() chooses without assuming CUDA. Training is about half an hour at these settings, and filling the 999 annotations into masks takes a couple of minutes before it. Nothing on this page depends on which card it was, except how long it took.
