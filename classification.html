Naming what a detector found

A detector’s crops, a classifier on top of them, and the class that is a statement about the annotation rather than about the picture.

A detector answers how many objects are there and where. A classifier answers what is this one picture of. Putting them in that order - find the objects, cut them out, hand the pieces to something that knows more classes than the detector does - is the second thing people ask for after boxes, and it is the one join this package deliberately leaves to you.

This article is that join, on BCCD, the same 364 photographs of blood smears the detection article uses. The detector is published, so nothing here has to find the cells again.

It does not end where it was meant to. The classifier learns the three cell types almost perfectly and fails completely at the one job it was added for, and the measurement that says why is the most useful thing on the page.

Where the numbers come from. Every figure below was produced by R’s vignette("classification"), which runs this same sequence through rplatypus on one machine; by maju116/rplatypus#117 that vignette is the source and this article is the copy, so the two walk the same sections and reach them by different routes - a configuration here, constructors there.

Cutting the objects out, which is a decision about the data

DetectionEngine.crops() returns arrays, and a classification run reads a directory or a CSV. The step in between - writing the crops out - is not in this package, and crops()’s own docstring says why: which crops are worth keeping is a decision about the data rather than about either model.

BCCD arrives in Pascal VOC’s layout, one flat directory of images beside one flat directory of XML, so a CSV per split is the only wrangling - the detection article has those dozen lines. With them, the published detector loads rather than trains:

import pyplatypus as pp

published = pp.from_dict(
    {
        "task": "object_detection",
        "data": {
            "train_path": "bccd-train.csv",
            "validation_path": "bccd-val.csv",
            "test_path": "bccd-test.csv",
            "classes": ["RBC", "WBC", "Platelets"],
            "mode": "config_file",
        },
        "models": [
            {
                "name": "cells",
                "architecture": "yolo3",
                "input_shape": [416, 416],
                "weights": "bccd-yolo3",
                "fit": False,
            }
        ],
    }
)
detector = pp.build_engine(published)
detector.fit()

records = detector.crops("cells", "test", size=(96, 96))

One record per image, with key, crops, boxes, scores, labels, names and dropped. size letterboxes rather than stretches, so a tall box keeps its shape - and it also throws away the one thing that separates these three classes most cleanly. The median side of an annotated object on the test split is 41 pixels for a platelet, 103 for a red cell and 182 for a white one, a factor of four; brought to 96 x 96 they are all the same size.

A crop needs a label, and the label is a decision

Each crop is labelled by what it overlaps: the best unclaimed annotated object at an overlap of at least 0.5 gives its class, and a crop that matches nothing gets a fourth class. Naming that class rather than dropping those crops is the decision, and ClassificationData argues for naming - there is no background entry, so a picture of nothing in particular is a class somebody has to name. Dropping them would train a classifier that believes every crop it will ever see is a cell.

Written out as one directory per class, following the dataset’s own three-way split, that is:

split RBC WBC Platelets nothing crops nothing
train 2367 214 209 129 2919 4.4%
validation 787 87 76 380 1330 28.6%
test 638 71 64 312 1085 28.8%

Read the last column before anything else. On the images the detector trained on, 4.4% of its crops match no annotated object; on the two splits it has never seen, 28.6% and 28.8%. A detector’s output on its own training images is not the output it will give you, and the factor here is about six.

What the detector already answers

Before training anything it is worth asking what a second model could add. Of the crops that do match an annotated object, how often was the detector’s own class right? 2790 of 2790 on the training split, 950 of 950 on validation, 772 of 773 on test.

One wrong out of 1,723 on the two splits the detector never trained on. A detector’s class head sees the object at the scale it occurs, with the whole photograph around it; a classifier sees a 96 x 96 crop with the scale normalised away. So naming the three cell types again is not work worth doing, and what is left is the other column: between a quarter and a third of what the detector hands over matches no annotated object at all. The question this page can usefully ask is not what is this crop but is this crop anything.

The classifier, and the metric that is not accuracy

task: classification
data:
  train_path: crops/train
  validation_path: crops/valid
  test_path: crops/test
  mode: nested_dirs
  classes: [RBC, WBC, Platelets, nothing]
models:
  - name: cells
    input_shape: [96, 96]
    blocks: 3
    filters: 16
    block_width: 2
    epochs: 60
    batch_size: 32
    optimizer: {name: adam, learning_rate: 0.001}
    augmentation:
      - {name: HorizontalFlip, params: {p: 0.5}}
      - {name: VerticalFlip, params: {p: 0.5}}
      - {name: RandomRotate90, params: {p: 0.5}}
    metrics: [{name: auc}, {name: balanced_accuracy}, {name: f1}]
    callbacks:
      - {name: cosine_annealing}
      - {name: early_stopping, monitor: val_auc, patience: 15, restore_best: true}
seed: 1

No encoder, so the contracting path is this package’s own; block_width rather than another level, because halving a 96-pixel crop four times leaves a platelet with nothing, and a crop of one cell is the case that lever is for.

ClassificationEngine.balance() is worth reading before the fit rather than after it, because the prevalence is what every later decision turns on: 81.1% RBC, 7.3% WBC, 7.2% Platelets, 4.4% nothing, and the engine’s own remark under the table says that below about 10% a metric read off the hard prediction stops telling a model from a collapse. That is why auc is the default here and accuracy is offered rather than chosen.

Training AUC reaches 0.9971 against a validation AUC of 0.8816, and the loss is the clearer half: the training loss falls to 0.047 while the validation loss rises from 1.54 to 1.94. The model is memorising crops. Early stopping restored epoch 29 of 44 - the best val_auc, which is what it was told to watch.

The best_epoch the scoring table reports is 18 instead, because that column is the lowest validation loss, read off the history whatever the callback was watching. The scores in that row are the restored epoch’s and the column names another one; pyplatypus#398 asks which of the two it should be.

Scoring per class, because the mean is the commonest class

evaluate("test") reports AUC 0.8727, balanced accuracy 0.7284 and F1 0.6817, and an accuracy of 0.730 - against 0.588 for a model that answers RBC to everything. That pair is the whole argument for the default metric: a reader told only the first would believe something had been learned about all four classes.

The confusion says where it went, with the truth down the side:

RBC WBC Platelets nothing
RBC 637 0 0 1
WBC 0 71 0 0
Platelets 0 1 52 11
nothing 253 5 22 32

The three cell types are nearly perfect. The fourth class is a rout: 32 of 312, with 253 of them called a red cell.

Two things that do not help, measured rather than assumed

A second seed of the same recipe, and the loss that exists for exactly this problem - focal cross-entropy, which loses interest in what it already gets right:

arm auc balanced accuracy share of alarms rejected
seed 1 0.8727 0.7284 0.103
seed 2 0.8657 0.7459 0.093
focal 0.8803 0.7238 0.051

Two seeds of one recipe differ by 0.0071 of AUC and focal loss moves it by 0.0076, which are the same order of magnitude - and two runs are not a spread, so neither figure is worth a conclusion on its own. The column that can carry one is the last: the share of the detector’s unmatched boxes that the model rejects runs from 0.05 to 0.10 across all three, which is to say that none of them does it.

The class that is not a picture of anything

The obvious repair is not to make the classifier choose but to ask it for a probability - predict() returns one row per image and one column per class - and reject a crop when nothing is likely enough. The threshold is a decision about cost, so it is chosen on the validation split and only then read on the test split. Precision before any of it is 0.7124, the share of what the detector hands over that is an annotated object; the best any threshold buys on the test split is 0.7482, at the cost of 4.3% of the real cells. A gain of 0.036 of precision for 33 cells, from a second model, a second dataset and a second training run.

And a second model has to be worth more than the one number the first one already has. Scoring the detector’s own boxes at a range of operating_points, with nothing trained:

operating_point boxes cells kept precision
0.50 1085 1.000 0.7124
0.70 1028 0.972 0.7305
0.80 990 0.955 0.7455
0.90 942 0.928 0.7611
0.95 907 0.899 0.7663
0.99 773 0.811 0.8111

The first row reproduces the 1,085 crops, which is the control that this is the same set of predictions read a second way. At 0.8 the detector keeps 0.955 of the cells against the classifier’s 0.957, at a precision of 0.7455 against 0.7482. A number that is already in the specification is on the same curve as a second model, and that is the comparison a page like this owes before it recommends anything.

So the rejector does not work, and the interesting question is why not. It is not the threshold and not the loss. It is the label. Of the 312 unmatched boxes on the test split, 92 overlap no annotated object whatsoever, 182 barely touch one, 31 are off their object and 7 are a second box on an object some higher-scoring box already took. And they are not guesses: their median score is 0.9864, against 0.9999 for the boxes that do match.

Looking at them settles it. They are cells - red cells whole and overlapping, out-of-focus smudges of exactly the kind the platelet class is made of, and patches of the purple a white cell is stained with. nothing is not a picture of anything; it is a statement about the annotation, and the question is this cell written down in the XML is not answerable from a picture of the cell.

One photograph makes the point better than the table does: on BloodImage_00327 the annotation has 2 objects and the detector finds 16 above its operating point, and on BloodImage_00249 it is 3 against 16. The extra boxes are red cells. BCCD annotates a subset of what is in some of its photographs, and a model trained on 205 images of it learned to find cells rather than to find annotations - which is the right thing to have learned and the reason a quarter of its output scores as wrong.

What this costs beyond this page: a precision measured against an incomplete annotation is a lower bound rather than an estimate, so the per-class precision in the detection article understates the detector by an unknown amount.

What this does not do

It does not show a classifier earning its place. On this dataset the detector already names its objects correctly 99.94% of the time, so the second model has nothing to add. Where one does earn its place is where the classes are finer than the detector’s: a detector that finds cells and a classifier that names twenty kinds of them, trained on crops whose labels come from somewhere other than the boxes. The surface is the same one; only the labels would differ.

It is not a measurement of the annotation. That a detector finds cells no annotation mentions says the annotation is incomplete on those images; how incomplete, on which images and for which class is a question somebody would have to answer by looking, and nothing here did.

The crops lose the scale. Every one is 96 x 96 whatever it was cut from, so the single most separable property of these three classes is gone before the classifier sees anything. size=None keeps each crop as cut, and then they cannot be stacked into one batch without a decision of your own.

It is one small collection. 205 training photographs at one magnification, stained one way, and the three classes it holds are easy to tell apart by eye. It is not a diagnostic tool, and a result from it is not a laboratory result.

Notes on the machine

A GTX 1070: the detector is loaded rather than trained, cutting and writing 5,334 crops takes about a minute, and each of the three classifiers is about two minutes. answers() returns a row per image with the label and the chosen class, predict_labels() the labels alone and predict() the probability matrix the thresholds above are read from.
